About Me

My name is RuoHan Yangyang Liu Luo, and I am currently pursuing a Master's in Clinical Neuroscience at Oxford University under the supervision of Prof. Ben Seymour . Prior to this, I completed a Bachelor of Science at University College London (UCL) .


My research interests lie at the intersection of systems, cognitive, and computational neuroscience. I aim to understand how the human brain operates as a whole. I believe there is strong potential in using computational modelling and machine learning tools to accelerate our understanding of neural network connections and underlying mechanisms. At the same time, I consider it essential to ground such approaches in empirical data if we are to move closer to understanding human intelligence rather than merely artificial intelligence.


During 2024–2025, I undertook a research visit at the DiCarlo Lab at MIT . This opportunity allowed me to study the ventral visual stream—and object recognition more broadly— through both experimental and computational approaches. The techniques I acquired during this period were unique and invaluable.

Besides my academic work, I enjoy taking on new challenges and learning new skills in the process. In 2024, I co-founded a startup called WildPals , which addresses one of the most persistent challenges in outdoor activities such as cycling and climbing: organizing group events. The app uses algorithmic learning to analyze user preferences, availability, and location, then intelligently matches people with relevant sporting events, classes, and social activities in their city. By streamlining this process, WildPals makes it much easier for clubs and communities to organize activities compared to relying on traditional social media platforms.

🌱 My Digital Garden

My research interests lie at the intersection of explainable artificial intelligence and cognitive neuroscience, with the aim of decoding both artificial and biological minds. I’m especially fascinated by the challenge of peering inside the black box of large-scale neural networks, not just analyzing their input-output behavior, but understanding the internal representational logic they develop over time.
This is, is probably one of the hardest if not impossible questions of this era, but that doesn’t make it less interesting. What does it mean when a neural network “understands” a concept — and how does that process compare to cognition in the human brain?
